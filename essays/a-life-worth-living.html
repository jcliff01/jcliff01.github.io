A Life Worth Living, Not Just Retiring From

On freedom, finite time, meaningful work, and the things that make a life rich.

By Jake Hilber · November 2026

We spend much of our lives learning how to earn a living. We become skilled, take responsibility, solve problems, and measure progress in promotions, projects, and financial milestones. Yet the most important question can remain unanswered: What is all this effort for?

Time is the one resource we cannot replenish. Money can be earned again. A failed project can be rebuilt. A career can change direction. But a year spent waiting for life to begin is a year we cannot recover.

I have spent much of my professional life thinking about complex systems: how they operate, why they fail, and how to make them more resilient. Increasingly, I find myself applying the same questions to life itself. What am I optimizing for? What are the hidden tradeoffs? And what would success actually look like?

Freedom is the point of financial independence

Financial security matters. It creates choices, reduces anxiety, and gives us the ability to help people we care about. But wealth is not a destination with a finish line. There will always be a larger number to pursue.

At some point, the value of another dollar must be weighed against the value of another day spent freely. The goal is not simply to accumulate assets. It is to build enough independence to choose how we spend our attention.

That might mean working fewer hours, taking a calculated professional risk, traveling longer, or being present when someone needs us. A good financial plan should make those possibilities more real—not indefinitely postpone them.

Health is the infrastructure of experience

Freedom means little if we neglect the body and mind that allow us to enjoy it. Energy, mobility, curiosity, and the capacity to learn are not peripheral concerns. They are foundational.

It is easy to imagine that meaningful adventures belong to a distant retirement. But different stages of life offer different abilities and opportunities. Some experiences are best pursued while we have the energy and health to embrace them fully.

Investing in sleep, movement, friendships, and mental well-being is not time taken away from achievement. It is what makes sustained achievement—and enjoyment—possible.

The world is larger than our routines

Earth is an extraordinary place, and our time to explore it is limited. There are landscapes we have never seen, cultures we have yet to understand, and people whose perspectives could change our own.

Travel is more than collecting destinations. It is a way to challenge assumptions and rediscover the scale of human experience. A life spent only inside familiar routines risks becoming efficient at the expense of being fully lived.

Contribution gives freedom direction

I do not believe the answer is to abandon work or ambition. The most satisfying kind of freedom may be the freedom to work on problems we genuinely care about.

Engineering, artificial intelligence, energy, and scientific discovery present opportunities to improve life far beyond our immediate surroundings. Experience accumulated over decades can become more valuable when it is directed toward a mission rather than simply a job title.

Meaningful contribution need not require sacrificing every waking hour. It may come from focused projects, mentorship, collaboration, or building something useful that outlasts our involvement.

People are not an interruption

Relationships are easy to treat as something we will make time for once the work is finished. But the work is never entirely finished.

Family, friendship, generosity, and shared experiences are not rewards waiting at the end of a productive life. They are part of its purpose. Helping someone through a difficult period or spending an ordinary afternoon together can matter more than another impressive accomplishment.

Build a life you do not need to retire from.

A better definition of success

Perhaps success is not the maximum possible net worth, the most prestigious title, or the longest list of achievements. Perhaps it is a life with enough security to feel free, enough health to remain curious, enough purpose to stay engaged, and enough love to feel connected.

That does not mean ignoring the future. It means refusing to sacrifice the present automatically in its name.

I want to keep learning, solving difficult problems, exploring this planet, and helping the people around me. I want to contribute to the future without forgetting to experience the world that already exists.

In the end, the question is not simply how much we accomplished. It is whether we used our limited time consciously—whether we built something, loved someone, saw something beautiful, and left the world a little better than we found it.
