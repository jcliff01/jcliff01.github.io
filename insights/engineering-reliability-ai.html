Engineering Reliability in the Age of Artificial Intelligence

Why production experience, systems thinking, and human judgment matter as infrastructure becomes more intelligent.

By Jake Hilber · Principal Engineer

Over two decades in enterprise infrastructure engineering, I have learned that reliable systems are not accidental. They are built through deliberate tradeoffs among availability, security, complexity, cost, and the people who depend on them.

Early in my career, supporting municipal technology taught me that an outage is never just a technical event. It can interrupt a public service or prevent someone from doing essential work. That lesson stayed with me as my work expanded into enterprise networking, unified communications, contact centers, and complex infrastructure environments.

In production, the hardest problems rarely respect organizational boundaries. A communications failure might involve routing, carrier connectivity, authentication, application behavior, or a subtle dependency between them. Solving it requires more than knowing a single product. It requires a mental model of the system, evidence-based troubleshooting, and clear coordination across teams.

Those principles are directly relevant to AI infrastructure. AI applications depend on dependable compute, networks, storage, data pipelines, access controls, observability, and operational processes. A capable model cannot compensate for an unreliable platform. Reliability, security, and cost discipline must be designed into the system rather than added after deployment.

I am especially interested in intelligent operations: systems that correlate telemetry, highlight anomalies, and help engineers investigate incidents faster. The objective is not to replace engineering judgment with an opaque answer. It is to give operators better context, explicit uncertainty, traceable evidence, and safe options for action.

Consider an enterprise communications environment supporting a hospital. A useful AI-assisted operations system might correlate SIP errors, network latency, carrier events, and application alarms into a coherent incident narrative. It would need to distinguish correlation from causation, protect sensitive data, and keep humans in control of consequential changes. Those are engineering challenges as much as AI challenges.

My direction is to bring production reliability, architectural judgment, and systems thinking into modern software and AI-assisted engineering. I am deepening my work in automation, software development, and data-driven operations while continuing to draw on the lessons of critical enterprise systems.

The next generation of technology will need more than impressive demonstrations. It will need infrastructure that works under pressure, failures that can be understood, and systems that earn trust through predictable behavior. That is the kind of engineering I want to help advance.

About the author

Jake Hilber is a principal engineer with more than 20 years of experience across enterprise infrastructure, networking, and communications. His current interests include AI-assisted operations, resilient systems, and engineering automation.
